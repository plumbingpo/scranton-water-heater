Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Taylor

A little routine care makes all the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode inspection every few years, a quick relief valve test once a year, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Equipment age plays a big role. With regular maintenance, a tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like rust-colored water, rumbling, or a damp spot around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and causes far less stress than an emergency swap with water spreading across the floor.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored for a few years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and burn out the lower element on an electric model, forcing a replacement much sooner. Common Water Heater Problems in Taylor

Most water heater problems we see in Taylor come back to what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the protective anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
